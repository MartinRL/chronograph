## D1 — Agentic Engineering

**"Nobody should type code by hand anymore" — what's left is the loop.** Geoffrey Huntley and Josh Wulf's four-episode deep dive on [loop engineering](https://ghuntley.com/agency/) crystallizes a position that's been building all year: the models stopped being the constraint long ago. What remains is vision (knowing *what* to build), verification (proving it works), and agency (the human's judgment about when to intervene). Huntley's prescriptive advice — delete your skill packs, put the whole system under test, and revisit everything that used to be hard — is essentially a call to relocate engineering effort from code production to spec and verification. This is D1 at its most opinionated, and it also maps directly to D4: without verification, the 2× output creates 4× downstream chaos.

**Anthropic's dynamic workflows borrow from Unix to coordinate agent swarms.** At AI Engineer New York, Anthropic [presented their architecture](https://www.latent.space/p/claude-dynamic-workflows) for coordinating multiple Claude agents on larger projects. The key design insight is drawing from Unix/Linux composability — small, single-purpose agent processes orchestrated through well-defined interfaces rather than monolithic mega-agents. This pattern matters for D1 practitioners because it offers a concrete alternative to the "one agent to rule them all" approach that tends to produce brittle, hard-to-debug workflows. The Unix analogy also implies pipes, permissions, and process isolation — all of which are prerequisites for bounded autonomy. (Cross-cutting: D3, as the composability model implicitly defines agent-to-agent interfaces.)

**Simon Willison builds micro-tooling to keep credentials out of the LLM loop.** Willison's [web-edit 0.1a0](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/12/web-edit/) is a small but telling artifact: while working from his phone via Codex Remote, he needed to edit a credentials file without round-tripping secrets through the LLM. So he had Codex and GPT-6 Astra Extra High build a web-based file editor accessible over Tailscale. The pattern here — building bespoke tooling *around* the agent to handle what the agent shouldn't touch — is becoming a recurring theme in mature agentic workflows. It's a practical illustration of the "human on the loop" model: the human doesn't write the code, but they architect the security boundary.

**QCon AI New York 2026 signals that production-grade agent engineering is now a conference-worthy discipline.** The [confirmed session lineup](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/qcon-ai-newyork-2026-sessionsv2/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=AI%2C+ML+%26+Data+Engineering) covers evals, LLM cost management, agent permissions, and guardrails — all problems that only emerge once agents are in production at scale. The fact that these topics now anchor a dedicated conference track (not a sidebar) confirms that agentic engineering has moved from experimental to operational. (Cross-cutting: D4, with sessions specifically on token cost management.)

## D2 — AI in the Product

**Joy Ebertz on the architect's evolving role in AI-driven development.** In a [podcast conversation with InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/podcasts/what-really-mean-program-ai/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=AI%2C+ML+%26+Data+Engineering), Ebertz argues that the most neglected task of a software architect — focusing on long-term business success — becomes even more critical as AI reshapes development. When code generation is cheap and fast, the architectural decisions about *what* to build and *how it evolves* become the primary value creators. This is a useful counterpoint to the "AI replaces developers" narrative: the role shifts from construction to judgment, which is precisely where D2 (what you embed in the product) intersects with organizational design.

## D3 — Build for Agents

**Anthropic's Unix-inspired agent coordination model has implicit A2A implications.** The [dynamic workflows architecture](https://www.latent.space/p/claude-dynamic-workflows) described by Anthropic, while primarily a D1 story, carries D3 signal: if agents are composed like Unix processes with well-defined interfaces, those interfaces become the de facto agent-to-agent protocol. Teams building for agent consumers should watch whether Anthropic formalizes these interfaces into something MCP-adjacent or keeps them proprietary.

## D4 — Cost of Ownership

**Spotify's 2× merge rate increase proves that AI velocity without downstream investment is borrowed speed.** This is the day's most important D4 story. [Spotify reports](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/spotify-ai-quality-controls/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=AI%2C+ML+%26+Data+Engineering) that AI-assisted development more than doubled their monthly merged changes — and that this forced a comprehensive review of quality and reliability controls. They describe changes across testing (scaling test coverage proportionally), observability (instrumenting the faster-changing surface area), rollback capacity (ensuring they can revert at the rate they deploy), workload prioritization, and automated maintenance. This is the 2×/½× equation in action: the 2× output arrived, but the ½× downstream cost required deliberate retroactive investment. Without it, the velocity would have produced more incidents, more support load, and more tech debt. Spotify's willingness to publicly document this adaptation makes it a reference architecture for any team scaling agentic development.

**AI inference costs are creating a software engineering cost crisis that FinOps can't yet handle.** Erik Peterson's [presentation on AI billing](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/ai-cloud-cost-economics/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=AI%2C+ML+%26+Data+Engineering) frames the problem starkly: AI spend is eclipsing cloud bills, and the tooling to attribute, forecast, and govern it doesn't exist at most organizations. He introduces the concept of "tokenmaxxing" — wasteful inference patterns that emerge when developers lack visibility into per-call costs — and argues that without unit economics discipline, fully autonomous AI (his 2028 prediction) will make the problem orders of magnitude worse. This is the "cost of the agentic factory" side of D4: the inference spend to run the agents that build your software is itself becoming a material line item.

**SRE Weekly spotlights AI SRE as an emerging practice area.** [Issue #538](https://sreweekly.com/sre-weekly-issue-538/) features a piece on "Building Investigations: what it takes to build an AI SRE," alongside a sponsored framework for scaling AI adoption in engineering. The convergence of SRE and AI is creating a new cost center (agentic SRE tooling) but also a new cost *reducer* (automated incident investigation and remediation). The open question is whether AI SRE tools reduce mean-time-to-resolution enough to offset their own inference and integration costs.

## Software Civil Engineering Lens

Today's items provide strong evidence for the SCE thesis from multiple angles:

**Huntley's "vision, verification, agency" trinity maps almost perfectly to SCE's Specify → Verify → Apply lifecycle.** When he says [the models stopped being the constraint](https://ghuntley.com/agency/) and what's left is verification and judgment, he's describing the transition from craft (developer skill in writing code) to engineering discipline (formal verification of generated artifacts). His advice to "put the whole system under test" is the SCE equivalent of requiring structural calculations before construction begins.

**Spotify's experience is the strongest real-world evidence yet that the professionalization forcing function is real.** Their [2× merge rate](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/spotify-ai-quality-controls/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=AI%2C+ML+%26+Data+Engineering) created immediate pressure on testing, observability, and rollback — all of which are analogous to civil engineering's inspection, monitoring, and safety systems. The fact that these controls had to be *retrofitted* rather than being in place from day one illustrates exactly the gap SCE identifies: software lacks the codes and norms that would mandate these controls before the velocity increase was permitted. In civil engineering, you don't get to double your construction speed and then figure out inspection later.

**Anthropic's Unix analogy for agent coordination is an interesting SCE data point.** The [dynamic workflows model](https://www.latent.space/p/claude-dynamic-workflows) suggests that agent systems are converging on composability patterns that have formal, well-understood properties (pipes, isolation, permissions). This is the "material datasheets" pillar in action: knowing the behavioral envelope of each agent-process allows you to compose them with predictable outcomes, just as knowing material properties allows structural composition.

The gap that remains most visible today: **no one is talking about licensure or formal certification** for agentic systems. The controls Spotify added are voluntary and organization-specific. The industry is still relying on each company to discover the right quality gates independently, rather than having shared codes and norms. The professionalization is happening, but it's happening company by company rather than discipline-wide.
