## D1 — Agentic Engineering

**Autonomous model post-training by agent teams is now demonstrably feasible.** [Athanor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21055) is an open, agent-native system in which four Claude Opus 5.5 agents — each on an eight-GPU node — autonomously post-trained Qwen3-4B-Base to match or surpass the official instruct model on a held-out suite (48.2 vs 47.9), over a 104-hour campaign with zero human research guidance. The agents independently recombined known methods (SFT, RL, weight merges, self-distillation), wrote and iterated training code, and coordinated through a persistent research record. The system's architecture — frozen evaluation, managed jobs, decontaminated data, held-out suites the agents can't see — is itself a template for how to provide "bounded autonomy" in a research loop. This is the clearest evidence yet that today's agents have sufficient engineering and research ability for non-trivial autonomous R&D, though the bounded sandbox is doing critical work to keep it safe.

**Gemini 4 Argon agents complete Minecraft end-to-end, demonstrating 27-hour coherent multi-agent planning.** [A team of four Gemini 4 Argon agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22319) defeated the Ender Dragon in 27 hours 14 minutes, writing 2,783 programs autonomously, coordinating via in-game chat and shared memory, with no pre-assigned roles or human-written game logic. The 605 deaths along the way are telling — the agents' error-recovery and mutual item-retrieval strategies are directly analogous to what enterprise agentic systems need for long-horizon coding tasks. The researchers watched via a live dashboard but never intervened, a concrete instance of "human on the loop." Also relevant to D3 (the Mineflayer library as an agent-consumable interface).

**FDE-Gym benchmarks the gap between well-specified tasks and real-world delivery.** [FDE-Gym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22350) introduces 95 delivery tasks across 18 sectors where agents must interact with scripted stakeholders, operate in production-like systems, and — critically — discover unstated constraints. The best of 13 model configurations achieves only a 0.56 mean score and resolves just 14 of 95 tasks. Even when models obtain all key facts from stakeholders, they only reach acceptable performance on 10 of 23 such tasks. This benchmark quantifies the "last mile" problem: agentic engineering tools are good at well-specified work but still struggle with the ambiguity-laden reality of customer delivery. Cross-cuts D4 (understanding where agents fail is essential to estimating downstream support costs).

**REA connects coding agents to binary analysis at scale.** [Reverse Engineer Anything](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/reverse-engineer-anything/?utm_campaign=infoq_content&utm_source=infoq&utm_medium=feed&utm_term=AI%2C+ML+%26+Data+Engineering) (200K+ GitHub stars) bridges Claude Code and Codex to Ghidra, Hopper, and IDA, enabling agents to inspect compiled binaries, trace function calls, and reverse-engineer feature implementations. For engineering teams dealing with legacy systems or third-party integrations, this extends agentic capabilities into previously human-only territory. Cross-cuts D3 (the tool-bridge pattern is itself an agent-consumable interface).

## D2 — AI in the Product

**Agent authorization is fundamentally broken in most runtimes.** [ASU (Agent Step-Up)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21063) audits ten agent runtimes and finds zero third-party verifiable approval records, no flood limits, and post-approval argument changes in four of eight runtimes with measurable binding. The paper defines twelve attacks on agent approval paths (decoy recipients, amount cancellation, threshold splitting, etc.) and tests them against AP2, Microsoft's action-bound design, and MCP elicitation. Their reference defense — a gateway that renders approval cards from canonical effect descriptors, signs them with a passkey, and executes only the stored call — is formally verified in Tamarin (53 lemmas). With the trusted card, forged-narration attack success drops from 0.54 to 0.03. Any product embedding agents in high-stakes workflows (payments, file operations, external communications) needs to treat this as a security-critical gap. Cross-cuts D3 and D4 (incidents from broken approval = major downstream cost).

**FDE-Gym also functions as a product benchmark for AI delivery platforms.** [The benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22350) includes an online platform where humans practice FDE skills under the same grading criteria, creating a direct human-AI comparison surface. For teams building AI-powered professional services or delivery automation products, the normalized business-uplift metric and stakeholder-interaction penalties provide a concrete evaluation framework.

## D3 — Build for Agents

**Agent Status Protocol (ASP) proposes a multi-dimensional state reporting standard.** [ASP v0.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21538) defines a lightweight protocol for heterogeneous agents to report operational state via HTTP POST JSON events, with SSE for observers. Its key design insight is modeling state along independent dimensions (lifecycle, health, task, floor, governance) so an agent can be simultaneously online, degraded, working on a task, and waiting for the floor. Critically, it doesn't trust agent self-reports alone: the server infers liveness from missed heartbeats and accepts observations from supervisors and peers with precedence rules. It reuses A2A task states, Open Floor Protocol floor states, and OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions. This is exactly the kind of interop-first observability standard that multi-agent deployments need, and the vocabulary reuse lowers adoption cost. Cross-cuts D4 (directly addresses agent observability as a downstream cost concern).

**SSR solves agent discovery scaling with a training-free retrieval approach.** [Structural Signal Retriever](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21113) addresses the problem that as agent registries grow, exposing the full catalog to an LLM orchestrator becomes prohibitively expensive and risks context-window overflow. At 1,000 agents, SSR reduces LLM input by 98.8% and orchestration latency by 70.9% using deterministic IDF-weighted signals from Agent Card fields and an Inverted Signal Index. It's training-free and traceable — important properties for regulated environments. Full-catalog orchestration showed "operational failures" at scale, meaning this isn't premature optimization; it's a prerequisite for production multi-agent ecosystems.

**ASU exposes critical gaps in MCP and A2A approval paths.** As noted in D2, the [ASU paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21063) specifically tests MCP elicitation and the Agent Payments Protocol, finding binding failures that matter for any B2A or A2A payment flow. The formal verification of effect binding and non-repudiation properties sets a bar that protocol designers building agent-facing APIs should meet.

## D4 — Cost of Ownership

**JevAgentHarness demonstrates that delegating routine decisions to a 4B model cuts agent inference costs by 81–89% without meaningful accuracy loss.** This directly enables the 2× output at ½× cost equation. [JevAgentHarness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21412) observes that most LLM agent steps end in a multiple-choice decision (which tool to call next) and trains AgentDecision-4B on agent trajectories — including error states — with Jev's answers as labels. A fitted confidence threshold per choice type determines when the small model handles the decision vs. escalating to the frontier LLM. On in-distribution tasks, this yields 81.1–89.0% cost reduction and up to 2.5× speedup with less than 3 points of task success loss. On out-of-distribution tasks, success holds at 91.5%. The key insight is that existing decision models fail at 69.5% on live agent tasks because they've never seen post-error states — training on actual agent trajectories closes this gap.

**Meta's Diviner formalizes agentic RCA into a four-stage lifecycle that directly attacks incident resolution costs.** [Diviner](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21281) (operating in production at Meta) explicitly addresses why generic LLM agents fail at root-cause analysis: stale context, misinterpreted failed queries, unsupported state claims, and overloading observability infrastructure. Its Encode→Execute→Evaluate→Adapt lifecycle separates skill versioning (scoped, owner-approved knowledge updates) from investigation execution (context-isolated workers maintaining evidence-linked hypotheses). The Evaluate stage compares trajectories to responder-reviewed outcomes, measuring "Groundedness" — whether conclusions are supported by evidence. The Adapt stage admits knowledge updates only after replay and quality checks. This is the most mature example yet of applying engineering discipline to agentic operations tooling, directly targeting the incident-response cost tier.

**FAOPs make agent workflow integrity, isolation, and cost bounds compile-time concerns.** [FAOP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22061) from JPMorgan applies four progressive normal forms to agent operating procedures: decomposed (atomic steps), typed (explicit data flow), factored (single canonical definitions for shared procedures), and scoped (least-privilege tool access). The algebra enables provably sound refactoring and parallelization as compiler rewrites, with extensions for verified termination and time/cost bounds. SkillBase (their management system) plus a runtime executor enforces these guarantees. For regulated sectors, this transforms auditability and cost bounding from "runtime hopes" to compile-time properties — exactly the kind of structural guarantee that reduces maintenance burden and audit costs downstream.

## Software Civil Engineering Lens

Today's batch is one of the strongest SCE signals we've seen. Multiple papers independently converge on the same thesis from different angles:

**FAOPs are building codes for agent workflows.** The [four normal forms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22061) (FNF-1 through FNF-4) are directly analogous to structural engineering codes: they define progressive levels of formal discipline that a workflow must satisfy before deployment. The compile-time termination verification (+V) and cost bounding (+B) extensions map precisely to the SCE concept of simulation before construction. JPMorgan building this for regulated finance validates that the professionalization pressure is real and coming from the sectors with the strongest compliance requirements.

**AgentEval introduces contract-bound assessment records.** [AgentEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21177) separates "what the agent did" from "what the evidence establishes" from "whether the conclusion is supported for present use" — a tripartite distinction that mirrors how civil engineers separate construction activity from material testing from structural certification. The concept of evidence views with binding-fault detection (36 faults missed by hash-only checks) is essentially materials testing for agent outputs.

**Phase-separated completeness auditing reveals a fundamental tension in spec-driven development.** The [Verus paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20676) demonstrates that correctness feedback — the thing that makes agents write code that passes tests — actively suppresses the evidence needed to find specification gaps. Phase-Revised contracts achieve 81.3% implementation success vs. 62.5% for iterative-feedback-revised contracts. This is a non-obvious finding with direct SCE implications: the Specify→Verify loop must preserve failure witnesses before correcting them. In civil engineering terms, you don't demolish the failed test specimen before analyzing why it failed.

**FDE-Gym quantifies the specification gap.** [The benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22350) shows that even when agents have all the facts, they resolve only 10 of 23 tasks adequately. This is evidence that the bottleneck isn't execution but specification comprehension — the ability to transform ambiguous requirements into actionable constraints. It argues strongly for Event Modeling or similar specification languages as prerequisites for agent-driven delivery.

**Diviner's lifecycle is Specify→Plan→Verify→Apply→Observe in practice.** [Meta's system](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21281) implements Encode (specify), Execute (plan+apply), Evaluate (verify), Adapt (observe and update) with human governance gates. The requirement that knowledge updates pass replay and quality checks before admission is exactly the "simulation before deployment" principle.

The needle moved today. We're seeing formal methods, progressive normalization, evidence-bounded assessment, and phase-separated auditing all emerge in the same week — not from academia alone, but from Meta and JPMorgan production systems. The professionalization isn't coming; it's here.
